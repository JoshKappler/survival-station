Seasonal Planting & Garden Almanac

A general guide for a temperate Northern-Hemisphere climate (frost in winter, warm summers). Your exact dates depend on your last spring frost and first fall frost — learn those two dates for where you live; everything below is timed relative to them.

The two dates that matter most: Count planting times from your last spring frost (when it is safe to plant tender crops) and your first fall frost (when tender crops die). "Weeks before/after last frost" is how seed packets schedule everything.

Cool-Season Crops (tolerate light frost)

Plant in early spring (2–6 weeks before last frost) and again in late summer for a fall crop.

Warm-Season Crops (killed by frost)

Plant only after your last frost when soil is warm.

Survival-Priority Calorie Crops

If food security is the goal, prioritize crops that yield the most calories and store without refrigeration:

  • Potatoes — high calories per square foot; store in a cool, dark place.
  • Winter squash & pumpkins — store for months in a cool room.
  • Dry beans — protein; dry on the plant, shell, store airtight.
  • Corn (dent/flint) — dries for grinding into meal.
  • Onions & garlic — flavor + keep well when cured.
  • Cabbage, carrots, beets — root-cellar storage; cabbage also ferments (sauerkraut).

Simple Year-Round Rhythm (temperate)

SeasonDo
Late winterStart tomato/pepper/brassica seeds indoors. Plan beds. Order/save seed.
Early springDirect-sow peas, spinach, radish, carrots. Plant potatoes, onions.
After last frostTransplant tomatoes/peppers. Sow beans, corn, squash, cucumber.
SummerWater deeply, weed, harvest daily. Succession-sow lettuce/beans. Start fall brassicas.
Late summerSow fall cool-season crops. Begin curing onions/garlic.
FallHarvest and store squash, potatoes, roots before hard frost. Plant garlic. Save seeds.
WinterStore crops cool/dry. Maintain tools. Plan next year. Eat from the root cellar and canned goods.

Seed Saving Basics

Let the best plants of open-pollinated / heirloom varieties (not hybrids) go to seed. Dry seeds fully, label with name and year, and store cool, dark, and dry (a jar with a little rice as desiccant works). Most vegetable seeds stay viable 2–5 years. Beans, peas, tomatoes, peppers, and lettuce are the easiest to save.

Cross-check before you eat or rely on a harvest. Use the food & foraging guides in this library for canning, storage, and preserving. When in doubt about whether stored food is still safe, follow the USDA canning/storage guide.

🥩 Butchering — breaking down a carcass

Turning a whole skinned animal into cuts you can cook, store and preserve — cleanly, and wasting nothing.

💡 You don't need to be an expert. Meat is just muscle. Separate the big muscle groups by following the natural seams between them with your fingers and knife, and you'll end up with usable cuts. Keep everything clean and cold as you work.

Let it cool (and age, if you can)

Before cutting, let the carcass cool completely. If you can keep it between about 34–40°F (1–4°C) — a cold shed, a spring house, a cool night — hanging it for several days ages the meat, making it more tender and flavorful. If it's warm out, skip aging and cut and preserve right away.

⚠️ Temperature rules. Above ~40°F meat spoils and can grow dangerous bacteria. If you can't keep it cold, get straight to cooking, drying or canning. When in doubt, throw it out — spoiled meat can kill.

The cuts (deer-size animal)

Work down the hanging carcass or on a clean surface. From a four-legged animal you get, roughly:

  1. Backstraps (loins): the two long, tender muscles running along either side of the spine, on the outside. Slice them free from neck to hip — your best, quickest-cooking meat. Cut into steaks.
  2. Tenderloins: two short, very tender muscles inside the body cavity along the lower spine. Remove them first — don't let them dry out.
  3. Hindquarters (hams): the big rear legs — the most meat on the animal. Separate the muscle groups along their seams into roasts, or slice for steaks, or trim for grinding/stew.
  4. Front legs (shoulders): tougher; best as slow-cooked roasts, stew meat, or ground.
  5. Ribs & neck: tougher cuts for braising, stewing or grinding. Boil the neck and bones for broth.
  6. Trim: all the scraps and odd bits go into a "grind/stew" pile. Mix lean game with some fat for burgers and sausage.

As you cut

  • Trim away silver-skin (the tough silvery membrane), bloodshot meat, hair and any dirt.
  • Keep cuts whole until you're ready to use or preserve them — large pieces keep better than small.
  • Label or remember tender cuts (fast, hot cooking) vs tough cuts (slow, moist cooking).
  • Save the fat and bones — don't discard them. See rendering below.

Smaller animals & birds

Rabbits and squirrels break into the same basic pieces: two front legs, two hind legs (the meatiest), and the saddle (back). Birds: two legs (thigh + drumstick), two wings, and the breast. Joint them at the natural joints — cut through the soft cartilage, not the bone.

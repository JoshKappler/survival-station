🏹 Hunting basics & shot placement

How to put meat on the table: where animals are, how to get close, where to aim for a clean kill, and how to find what you hit.

⚠️ Gun & bow safety — always. Treat every weapon as loaded. Never point it at anything you are not willing to destroy. Be 100% certain of your target and what is behind it before you release. Keep your finger off the trigger until your sights are on the animal. A hunting injury far from help can be fatal.

Find the animals first

You hunt where animals already are, not where you wish they were. Look for the three things every animal needs — food, water and cover — and hunt the edges between them, especially at dawn and dusk when animals move to feed.

  • Sign: tracks, droppings (fresh = dark and moist), trails worn through grass, beds of flattened vegetation, rubs and scrapes, feathers or fur, chewed plants.
  • Water: in dry country, sit downwind of a spring, pond edge or creek crossing near last light.
  • Food: berry patches, acorn-bearing oaks, green field edges, garden raiders.
  • Wind: most game lives by its nose. Keep the wind in your face, blowing from the animal to you.

Get close: still-hunting and ambush

  1. Ambush (easiest): find fresh sign, sit still and silent downwind near a trail or water, and let the animal come to you. Patience kills more game than walking.
  2. Still-hunt: move into the wind a few slow steps at a time, then stop and look and listen for several minutes. Look for pieces of an animal — a horizontal line, an ear, an eye — not the whole shape.
  3. Stay low and quiet: avoid skylining yourself on ridges, step on quiet ground, and freeze when an animal looks your way.

Shot placement — aim for a fast, humane kill

Aim to destroy the heart and lungs, not the guts. On a broadside animal (standing sideways), put the shot tight behind the front shoulder, about one-third of the way up the body. This drops big and small game quickly and ruins the least meat.

  • Broadside: behind the near shoulder, lower-middle chest. Best shot.
  • Quartering away: aim to drive the shot forward through the chest toward the far shoulder.
  • Avoid: head/neck shots (small target, easy to wound), and the belly/hindquarters (a gut-shot animal runs far and suffers).
  • Small game: head shots with a .22 or shotgun preserve meat; with a shotgun, lead a moving bird and aim at the head/neck.
💡 Steady the shot. Rest the weapon on a pack, a tree, or a shooting stick. Breathe out, pause, and squeeze — never jerk — the trigger. A calm, rested shot beats a fast one.

After the shot

  1. Mark the spot where the animal stood and where it ran. Stay put and watch where it goes.
  2. Wait. Unless it dropped on the spot, give a chest-shot animal 20–30 minutes (longer if the hit looked far back). Pushing a wounded animal makes it run for miles.
  3. Read the blood. Bright pink frothy blood = lungs (good, it won't go far). Dark red = muscle/liver. Green/brown with stomach smell = gut hit; wait several hours, then track slowly.
  4. Trail carefully. Follow blood and tracks, marking your last sign so you can return to it. Look ahead for the animal bedded down.
  5. Confirm it has passed before approaching from behind/above; a downed animal can still kick or gore. Then field dress it immediately.
